*What if some of the smallest inhabitants of your body were quietly helping you digest food, defend against pathogens and regulate your immune system? The human body is not simply an individual organism; it is also a habitat for a complex community of microorganisms that interact with us in ways scientists are still discovering.*

## What is the microbiome?

The microbiome is the collection of all the microbes, including bacteria, viruses and fungi, that live in a specific environment, such as on the skin or in the digestive tract.

Trillions of microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses, colonise our gut, forming a dynamic ecosystem which is shaped by our diet, age, lifestyle and medication. Beyond aiding digestion and metabolism, these microbes interact with our immune system, influence gut–brain communication, and transform certain medicines.

**But what happens when this delicate relationship shifts?** Let’s explore the science connecting our microbial world to health, diseases and emerging therapies.

## How do gut microbes communicate with the immune system?

### An Alliance Within: How Gut Microbes Train Our Immune System

What if your gut held some of the keys to a better-balanced immune system? Trillions of microbes living in your intestine help train your body’s defences, shaping both innate and adaptive immunity from early life.

How does this partnership work? The intestinal lining forms a protective barrier against harmful substances, while gut microbes help strengthen it through biochemical signals. As microbes ferment dietary fibre, they produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which support the gut barrier and help regulate inflammation. These microbial signals also influence regulatory T cells (Tregs), which help prevent excessive immune responses, and immunoglobulin A (IgA), an antibody that helps control microbes at mucosal surfaces.

This partnership begins early: microbial exposure around birth, alongside breastfeeding, nutrition and environmental factors, helps shape immune development.

*Gut microbes do more than aid digestion* – they help the immune system defend against foreign offence while maintaining tolerance to harmless substances.

But their influence doesn’t stop at immunity. Could these microscopic partners also communicate with your brain and influence how it functions?

## The gut–brain axis: science, hype and the truth between

### The Connection That Blows Minds: Gut–Brain Connection

**Ever felt butterflies in your stomach when nervous?** It reflects a fascinating connection between your gut and brain. Scientists call it the gut–brain axis, a two-way communication network that helps regulate digestion and may also influence emotions, motivation and decision-making.

How does this conversation work? The gut sends information to the brain through sensory nerves, including the vagus nerve, as well as through hormonal and immune signals. Specialised cells in the intestinal lining, called enteroendocrine cells, detect gut conditions and release chemical messengers that can activate nearby nerves. Meanwhile, gut microbes may influence these pathways through their metabolic products and interact with the immune system.

![Diagram of the brain–gut–microbiome system: environmental exposures such as brain-targeted stress and dietary stress act on the central nervous system and the gut microbiome, which communicate in both directions through microbe-derived molecules, autonomic nervous system signals and enteroendocrine cells in the gut](images/brain-gut-microbiome-system.jpg "Schematic of the brain–gut–microbiome (BGM) system as interactions between the environment, gut microbiome, host enteric and immune system, autonomic nervous system, and brain. Research suggests that gut signals can affect how the brain processes bodily sensations and emotions. However, although animal studies offer intriguing clues about microbial effects on behaviour, the extent of these effects in humans remains uncertain. The connection is real, but many claims about microbes controlling our mood or behaviour are still being investigated.

## Your gut microbes: the hidden influencers of medicine

**Ever wondered why the same medicine might not work the same way for everyone?**

The answer may partly lie in our gut microbes. Pharmacomicrobiomics explores how medicines and gut microbes influence each other.

Gut bacteria can transform drugs, altering their activity and availability. Some bacteria can inactivate digoxin, while others convert levodopa into dopamine, reducing the amount available to reach the brain. In mice, blocking this bacterial enzyme increased levodopa levels in the bloodstream.

The relationship works both ways: medicines, especially antibiotics, can reshape gut microbial communities and their functions, potentially influencing future drug responses.

![Four-panel diagram of how gut bacteria affect drugs: absorption (bile acid micelles, transporters, bacterial compounds), metabolism (activation of pro-drugs and inactivation of drugs), excretion (removal of glucuronide and sulfate groups) and toxicity (formation of toxic drug metabolites and an altered microbiome)](images/microbes-drug-metabolism.jpg "Bacteria may affect drug absorption through bioaccumulation (uptake of the parent drug), bile salt metabolism (changes to emulsification/solubilisation of lipophilic drugs) or altered drug uptake through passive (paracellular) diffusion or protein carrier–mediated transport (influx and efflux). Bacteria can also metabolise drugs directly, produce metabolites that alter the expression and activity of host DMEs, and modify bile acids that act on host nuclear receptors (e.g. PXR). Bacteria may also convert host drug metabolites back into their active and toxic forms (e.g. via deglucuronidation or sulfatase activity), transform drugs into metabolites with local or systemic toxicity (toxification) or negatively alter gut microbiome composition (dysbiosis). | Source: Collected")

Understanding the hidden partnership between medicines and microbes could help researchers develop more personalised treatments, but translating these discoveries into better outcomes for patients will require further human studies.

## When microbial harmony falters: from gut imbalance to disease

**What happens when our microscopic allies fall out of balance?**

Dysbiosis – an alteration in the gut microbiome’s composition or its function – can disrupt the relationship between microbes and their host.

Factors such as antibiotics, infections and dietary changes can alter microbial communities. These changes may weaken intestinal barrier function, disturb immune regulation and promote inflammation. Certain pathogens cause direct harm; for example, Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* can trigger bloody diarrhoea and kidney complications, while *Clostridioides difficile* can cause severe colitis following antibiotic-induced microbiome disruption. *H. pylori* can cause peptic ulcers and increase gastric cancer risk.

Beyond infections, dysbiosis is associated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), metabolic disorders and colorectal cancer.

**But does dysbiosis cause disease, or does disease cause dysbiosis?**

Both are quite possible. Microbial changes may contribute to disease, while inflammation, altered diet, lifestyle and medications can further reshape the microbiome, creating a cycle that sustains illness. However, these relationships vary, and association alone does not prove causation.

Understanding this two-way relationship may reveal new opportunities for prevention and treatment.

## Can we reshape our gut microbiome to improve health?

From fibre-rich diets and probiotics to faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), scientists are exploring ways to restore microbial balance. FMT has strong evidence for recurrent *C. difficile* infection, while engineered bacteria and phage therapy offer promising future possibilities.

**The challenge?** Finding the right approach for the right person.
