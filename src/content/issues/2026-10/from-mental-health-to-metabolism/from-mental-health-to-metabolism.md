We often blame diet and a lack of exercise for bad metabolisms, whereas chronic psychological stress can alter our metabolism too while the diet remains unchanged. For years, mental health and metabolic health have been seen as two entirely separate fields. If you gained weight, it was a metabolic issue; if you felt depressed, it was a psychiatric one. But now many studies show that it is not the case. Conditions like major depression and chronic anxiety directly disrupt the body’s metabolic systems by triggering a cascade of cortisol, promoting cellular insulin resistance, and altering the gut–brain axis. To truly understand our metabolism, first we must look at what is happening in our minds. So let’s start with the physiology of it all.

## Cortisol: link between brain and metabolism

Cortisol is necessary for our body as well as our mind. It helps with lipolysis (breakdown of fat), proteolysis (breakdown of protein) and glycogenolysis (breakdown of glycogen), and it maintains blood sugar levels in our body by signalling the liver to release glucose and reducing glucose uptake in muscles when needed.

Cortisol also helps in maintaining the circadian rhythm and producing “fight or flight” mode as a stress response. As a result, chronic stress, anxiety, major depression, trauma or any imbalance in your emotional state keeps the Hypothalamo-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis turned on, which causes persistently high levels of cortisol in our body, leading to gluconeogenesis (producing excess glucose in the liver), insulin resistance and storage of fat, specifically around the abdomen, causing weight gain.[^1] High cortisol lowers fullness hormones (leptin and GLP-1) and elevates hunger hormones (ghrelin), which leads to faster, mindless eating and cravings for sweet, fatty and salty food.[^2] This means you are more likely to indulge in junk food than a healthy diet.

## Three pathways from stress to anxiety and depression

Beyond cortisol, researchers have described three routes that link stress to the brain.[^3] The first involves GDF15, a stress-responsive hormone that connects fat metabolism to anxiety circuits in the brainstem. The second runs through the gut–brain axis. Stress alters the gut bacteria and raises levels of ceramides, fatty molecules that can reach the brain and impair the mitochondria of the hippocampus. The third is mitochondrial dysfunction within the brain itself, where damaged cellular energy production in emotional regions may keep stress signalling switched on.

## The gut–brain axis and insulin resistance

The gut–brain axis is a two-way pathway between the brain and the gut. That is why changes in emotion affect the digestive system immediately, and vice versa. Did you ever have an exam coming up and suddenly feel sick to your stomach? That’s the gut–brain axis at work. Here the amygdala determines the stressor (the exam) and sends a signal to the hypothalamus, which activates the sympathetic nervous system. This system prioritises survival over digestion. The vagus nerve sends signals towards the enteric nervous system to slow gut motility, which reduces digestion. While upper stomach motility is reduced, stress hormones like cortisol and adrenaline hyperactivate the lower intestines, causing them to contract suddenly so that they can quickly expel waste.

When the stressful event is done, the parasympathetic nervous system takes charge, blood flow returns to the abdomen and the gut functions. Stress hormones decrease the production of protective mucus and weaken the tight junctions in the intestinal wall, allowing bacterial fragments like lipopolysaccharides (LPS) to slip into the bloodstream, causing a condition called “metabolic endotoxaemia”.[^3] They detect LPS by a security receptor called TLR4. When LPS binds to the receptor, the immune system believes the body is suffering from a blood infection. This releases cytokines (such as C-reactive protein, TNF and IL-6) into the bloodstream and causes low-grade inflammation, which spreads to every major organ, like the liver, muscle and fat, and interferes with the insulin receptor, causing insulin resistance,[^5] ultimately leading to metabolic dysfunction and type 2 diabetes.

## Medication side effects

Psychiatric conditions are associated with an increased risk of developing metabolic syndrome (MetS), which is a cluster of cardiovascular risk factors including dyslipidaemia, abdominal obesity, hypertension and hyperglycaemia. Certain psychiatric treatments, such as second-generation antipsychotics and specific antidepressants, can cause extensive weight gain and also facilitate most metabolic alterations, with weight gain reported in up to 72% of all antipsychotic-receiving patients. However, there is also evidence of metabolic alterations without any weight gain. Antipsychotics, especially clozapine and olanzapine, carry a very high risk of weight gain by destabilising fat tissue regulation. Because these drugs target dopamine D2 receptors, they alter brain pathways that control food intake while simultaneously disrupting receptors on the insulin-producing β-cells of the pancreas, directly impairing blood sugar control.[^6]

In Bangladesh mental health is dismissed as luxury or a simple overreaction. We hear people say “get over it” more times than it is taken seriously. It is not seen as a real health issue. Even those who understand it don't know the seriousness of it all. True well being requires taking care of both our minds and our bodies because they are deeply interconnected. To thrive, every individual needs a safe, supportive environment where they can open up, relax and find genuine peace of mind. So we should break the cycle of ignorance about mental health and recognize it as part of our health in order to ensure a positive lifestyle.
